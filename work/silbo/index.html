The brief

A playable vertical slice of a 2D game, in Unity, for PC and Mac, played on a gamepad and presented publicly at HIVE SUMMER — where visitors tested it in front of us. Judged on game feel, visual coherence, technical stability across a full session, and balancing proven through playtesting.

Silbo was built between May and July 2026 at HTW Berlin, as the joint project of three modules on our Game Design B.A. For all five of us it was the first game we had ever made in a team and carried to this level of finish.

It began not with a setting or a mechanic but with a Desiderata — a set of conditions any later idea would have to satisfy. The concrete image came from the Salto del pastor canario, the shepherd's leap of the Canary Islands, where herders vault across volcanic terrain on a long wooden staff. One practice gave us a protagonist, an environment and a gameplay focus at once.

What I did

Programming & technical art

  1. Tooling

    A crack-painting tool for the falling platforms

    They had to break along the cracks drawn in the artwork. Every existing solution we found was procedural, which gave us no control over the artist's exact lines — so we built a standalone Unity tool that lets you paint the cracks straight onto the platform and drives the break from that. It has since grown into an editor tool of its own.

  2. Workflow

    Tuning the whole team could reach

    I made a point of surfacing the variables that mattered in the Inspector, so designers and artists could tune a feature without touching code or waiting on a programmer. That is what made playtest days usable, since values changed on the spot and repeatedly.

  3. Performance

    Making it run on the weakest machine in the room

    The game ran badly on some of the Hive's computers. Profiling put the cost in our shaders and in the sheer volume of art on screen; optimising both is what kept it playable on something other than our own development machines — which mattered, because strangers played it on hardware we did not choose.

  4. Process

    AI in the loop, reviewed by hand

    We used it to plan and prototype quickly, and as a second pass over incoming work to flag edge cases — useful on critical systems like data storage, where a mistake spreads. Generated code was never assumed correct: it was read and judged before it shipped.

The modules covered design, art and technology at once, so everyone worked across their own discipline and most features passed through several hands. The level segments were divided among all five of us — the world the player runs through is literally built by the whole team.

1648 m — the corruption

Pursuit

When slow
Closes the gap — but never goes straight for the kill
Tells
Colour drains from the world; the ambience shifts

You are not being chased by a wall

The corruption is a difficulty system wearing a costume. It gains ground as the run goes on, growing more insistent with every kilometre covered and every goat handed in — so the reward for playing well is a pursuer that plays better too. It closes the gap when you are slow, but it does not go straight for the kill, which keeps a bad stretch tense instead of hopeless.

Long before it arrives, the sound and the colour of the world announce it: the warmth drains out of the landscape and everything shifts towards black and white. Learning to read that is part of learning the game — and it means the screen tells you that you are in trouble while your hands stay busy.

It was not always this. Early feedback flagged the wolves we had planned as the pursuer as a tired trope, so we replaced them with something deliberately unresolved — danger that reads through half-glimpsed figures rather than a defined creature.

Payout

Every verb is a link in a chain

None of these actions is interesting alone. That is the design: they exist to be strung together, and chaining them cleanly answers precision with momentum. Hold a combo alive and you are given a wind shield that destroys whatever is in your path, so the speed you built survives contact with the world.

That is the whole difference between a cautious run and a fast one. The game is at its best in the state where the terrain reads itself and every input lands where it should — and everything else is arranged to make that state reachable.

A language, used as an input

On La Gomera, shepherds talk across ravines too wide to cross in Silbo Gomero, a whistled form of Spanish. We built the mechanic first and realised only afterwards that it had been holding our title the whole time — for most of the project the working name was The Goat.

The whistle is what turns running through a landscape into communicating with one. It calls the herd, and it sets the world moving. Striking a wind chime and catching its ring mid-flight restores an aerial combo and pushes the corruption back for a moment, which makes a chain of chimes both a route and a reprieve.

The sound was tuned to be satisfying on its own, and the result is that players reach for the chimes before they understand what they do — the mechanic teaches itself through the audio rather than through a prompt.

The herd is your speed, and your risk

Goats make you faster, which makes the corruption easier to outrun — but a careless hit scatters part of the herd again, so the advantage is never entirely safe until you reach a checkpoint. Handing them in converts something fragile into something secure, and the run starts gathering from zero once more.

The moment is deliberately generous: a downhill stretch follows where the pursuit eases and you can breathe. It is the only rest the game gives, and it is given for playing well rather than for surviving.

The checkpoints themselves are stone structures covered in paintings — constructed shapes that stand apart from the natural rock as signs of civilisation in the wilderness. They show a relationship with the mountains and the herd without explaining the history behind them, leaving traces for the player to read instead.

A route assembled at runtime

The world is put together as you run from modular sections called chunks, so no two runs are the same. Each was blocked out and playtested for paths, jumps and flow before any art went in, then dressed with modular assets, given a single collider for the whole chunk, and finally populated with plants, shaders and animation.

The terrain punishes inattention: cacti, erupting geysers, plants that swing and knock you down — though those can be turned to your advantage — platforms that give way underfoot, and others that appear only when called.

Over the run the light moves from dawn through midday and evening into night. We added the cycle so the journey would feel longer and more meaningful without dividing the world into levels: with no level numbers and no map, the colour of the sky is the only progress bar there is.

The version we did not make

The earliest prototype was a different game. It had skill checks, and an AI goat the player could communicate with to change the terrain, perform movement abilities and solve small puzzles — all of it meant to combine into a loop with roguelite elements.

Coaching sessions warned us we were overscoping, and pushed us towards the player and the movement rather than the goat, the puzzles or individual levels. The suggestion that the player simply needed more distance to run is what turned the project into an endless runner. The roguelite, the AI goat and the puzzles fell away; the pursuit, the herd and the flow of movement stayed.

We worked prototype-first for exactly this reason. Where we were unsure about a feature we built it rather than argued about it, often several ways at once, and judged the versions by how they actually played. Playtesting was mostly watching people rather than handing them a form — from players with almost no experience to very experienced ones, since the goal was something easy to learn and hard to master.

The roguelite idea is not dead: the plan is to bring it back, with the goats you rescue as the currency you upgrade with before setting off again.
